# Why Predictions Fail: Risk, Uncertainty, and the Courage to Change Your Mind

Predictions fail for a simple reason: reality is usually more complicated than our models of it. We confuse measurable risk with deeper uncertainty, mistake correlation for causation, and become too confident in explanations that worked only in the past.

## Risk is measurable. Uncertainty is not so cooperative.

Risk describes situations where outcomes and probabilities are reasonably well known. We can estimate the chance of rain, crop failure, or a market loss from data and models. Uncertainty begins where those probabilities become difficult to measure: the outcomes may be unfamiliar, the system may be changing, or important variables may be missing.

This distinction matters because a precise number can create an illusion of knowledge. A forecast of 70% is useful only if we understand what was measured, which assumptions were made, and how often similar forecasts have been right.

## Out of sample, out of mind

A model can explain historical data beautifully and still fail when conditions change. This is the out-of-sample problem: the future contains combinations of events that were absent from the data used to build the prediction.

Climate provides a vivid example. In July 2026, the World Meteorological Organization reported [record-breaking heat and extreme weather](https://wmo.int/media/news/record-breaking-heat-and-extreme-weather-continue), including drought, wildfires, floods, and unusually high sea-surface temperatures.

But the details resist a simple headline. NOAA classified July 2026 as tied for the warmest July on record, while Copernicus ranked it second. Western Europe experienced its hottest June-July period, yet Europe as a whole recorded only its eleventh-warmest July because eastern Europe and Scandinavia were cooler than average.

These observations do not contradict one another. They use different datasets, methods, regions, and time periods. A statistic cannot be separated from how it was constructed. The long-term pattern often tells us more than a single ranking.

## Be a fox, not a hedgehog

The hedgehog knows one big thing and tries to explain everything through it. The fox knows many smaller things and is willing to combine them.

Good forecasters are foxes. They compare models, study base rates, seek conflicting evidence, and remain suspicious of explanations that are too elegant. They ask what else could produce the same result.

The WMO's [forecast for a strengthening El Nino](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/strong-el-nino-expected-intensify) takes this broader view. It considers El Nino alongside the Indian Ocean Dipole, Atlantic Ocean temperatures, and other climate drivers. It also states that El Nino's effects vary with intensity, duration, timing, and interactions with other systems.

Most importantly, its seasonal maps represent **probabilities, not certainties**. A forecast of above-normal rainfall does not promise rain in every location. It says that one outcome is more likely than the alternatives.

## Think probabilistically

A useful prediction should not merely say what will happen. It should tell us:

- how likely the outcome is;
- what range of outcomes is possible;
- which assumptions support the estimate;
- what evidence would change the conclusion; and
- what the consequences would be if the prediction were wrong.

Instead of saying, "This will happen," say, "Given the available evidence, this is the most likely outcome." Then update that belief as new evidence arrives.

Changing your mind when the facts change is not weakness. Refusing to update a prediction after its assumptions have failed is.

## Forecasts can change the future

Economic predictions are especially difficult because people respond to them.

The American Farm Bureau Federation estimates that [significant farm losses will persist despite federal assistance](https://www.fb.org/intel/markets/significant-farm-losses-persist-despite-federal-assistance). It projects rising production costs for nine major row crops and estimates sector-wide net losses of more than $50 billion across three crop years.

The report also includes an essential qualification: these are national averages calculated before farmers make their final planting, purchasing, and marketing decisions. Farmers may change their crop mix, reduce inputs, renegotiate costs, or adopt different risk-management strategies. Regional conditions and ownership structures also vary.

The forecast is not fate. It is a conditional estimate based on current assumptions. Those assumptions help people make decisions, and those decisions can alter the eventual outcome.

## Watch for magic bullets

When a problem is complex, a simple solution can be emotionally attractive. That does not make it effective.

Federal assistance may reduce immediate financial pressure on farms, but the Farm Bureau analysis suggests that it does not fully close the gap between costs and market returns. Likewise, a better weather forecast cannot prevent a flood or drought by itself. It creates an opportunity to prepare.

There is rarely one policy, model, or technology that eliminates uncertainty. Magic-bullet thinking usually ignores trade-offs, feedback loops, and unintended consequences.

## Causality is hard to sort out

When two things happen together, we naturally want to say that one caused the other. Correlation, however, is only the beginning of a causal argument.

Extreme weather can affect agricultural output, but farm profitability also depends on fertilizer, fuel, interest rates, labor, commodity prices, land costs, trade, and policy. Isolating one cause requires careful comparison, credible counterfactuals, and an honest account of what remains unknown.

"The model predicts it" is not a causal explanation. Neither is "these events happened at the same time."

## Better predictions begin with humility

We cannot eliminate uncertainty, but we can communicate it more honestly. We can publish ranges instead of false precision, attach probabilities to claims, document assumptions, and distinguish observations from interpretations.

The goal is not perfect certainty. It is better calibration: confidence when the evidence is strong, caution when it is weak, and a willingness to update when reality surprises us.

A prediction should be treated as a tool for making decisions, not as a promise about the future. The best forecasters are not those who never get anything wrong. They are those who understand how they might be wrong and make that uncertainty visible.
